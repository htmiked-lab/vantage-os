What BPA Is (and Why It Matters)

BPA — bisphenol A — is an industrial chemical used to make certain plastics and resins. For decades, it was used widely in baby bottles, sippy cups, and toys. Research has linked BPA exposure to hormone disruption, particularly in young children whose systems are still developing. The concern isn't abstract: BPA can leach from plastic into food, water, or saliva, especially when heated or scratched.

BPA-free became a standard expectation for kids products for good reason. But here's the catch: "BPA-free" doesn't automatically mean "safe." Some manufacturers replaced BPA with structurally similar chemicals (BPS, BPF) that may have comparable effects. A product can be technically BPA-free and still contain things you wouldn't want near your kid.

We avoid this whole category entirely by using silicone, which doesn't require bisphenols at all.

Why Silicone Is Our Answer

Food-grade silicone is a different class of material. It's not a plastic in the traditional sense. It doesn't require plasticizers, softeners, or bisphenols. It's chemically inert — meaning it doesn't react with food, water, or saliva, and doesn't leach compounds into whatever it touches.

Silicone is used in medical devices, hospital equipment, baby bottle nipples, and cookware because of this profile. It's heat-stable, UV-stable, and doesn't break down into microplastics the way traditional plastics do.

When we talk about BPA-free, non-toxic materials, silicone is what we mean — and we've chosen it for every product we sell.

The Other Chemicals to Watch For

BPA isn't the only concern in kids products. Our material standards explicitly exclude:

  • BPA, BPS, BPF — the whole bisphenol family
  • Phthalates — plasticizers used to soften plastic, linked to hormone disruption
  • PVC (polyvinyl chloride) — common in cheap bath toys, often contains phthalates
  • Lead and cadmium — heavy metals sometimes found in colorants and paints
  • Formaldehyde — found in some foam and composite materials
  • Toxic flame retardants — more common in soft furnishings than toys, but worth checking

Our silicone avoids all of these by design. No added phthalates. No PVC. No heavy metals in our colorants. No formaldehyde.

What "Food-Grade Silicone" Actually Means

Food-grade silicone is silicone that meets specific regulatory standards for direct food contact. In the U.S., that's FDA approval under 21 CFR 177.2600. In the EU, it's LFGB certification. These standards test for chemical migration — whether anything leaches out of the silicone into food, water, or saliva under various conditions.

We source silicone that meets both FDA and LFGB standards. That's why we can confidently put our products in your bath, at your beach, and in your kid's mouth. The material has been tested specifically for these uses.

Not all silicone on the market meets these standards. Cheap silicone may be "silicone-like" but contain fillers or additives that wouldn't pass food-contact certification. We don't use those suppliers.

Certified food-grade silicone is more expensive than generic silicone or standard plastic. This affects our product pricing. We could make cheaper products using less rigorous materials, and we've had suppliers pitch us on it. We've said no every time.

Our reasoning is simple: our products end up on kids' hands, in kids' mouths, and around kids' food. Compromising on materials isn't a trade-off we're willing to make for a lower price point. We'd rather charge fairly for a genuinely safe product than be one more company promising "non-toxic" while cutting corners on the definition.

How to Evaluate Kids Products Yourself

If you're shopping beyond Tiipikids (which, to be clear, you absolutely should — we're one brand, not the only one that cares), here's what to look for:

  • Specific certifications: FDA, LFGB, EN71 (European toy safety), CPSIA (U.S. children's product safety)
  • Material named clearly: "100% food-grade silicone" beats vague "BPA-free plastic"
  • Country of origin with quality standards: Know where the product is made
  • Third-party testing references: Reputable brands share their testing practices
  • Transparent ingredient or material lists — if it's vague, ask or skip

Be skeptical of vague labels. "Non-toxic" without specifics is marketing. "Food-grade silicone, FDA and LFGB certified" is a claim you can verify.

Beyond Materials: Durability as Safety

A product that breaks is a safety hazard, even if the original material was safe. Cheap plastic toys develop sharp edges as they degrade. Hollow toys grow mold inside. Crumbling plastic sheds microplastics into bathwater.

Part of our material commitment is choosing materials that stay safe throughout their use. Silicone doesn't develop sharp edges. It doesn't grow mold when used as a solid piece. It doesn't shed particles. Durability is a safety feature, not just a longevity one.

What We Don't Use — Even Though We Could

A few materials we've actively chosen to avoid despite being common in kids products:

  • PVC: Ubiquitous in cheap bath toys, often softened with phthalates
  • EVA foam: Used in bath foam letters and mats, can absorb water and grow mold
  • Polystyrene: Cheap and lightweight but brittle and prone to fracturing
  • Unspecified "BPA-free plastic": Often means BPS or BPF substitutes

These materials are legal. They're widely used. We just don't want to use them.
